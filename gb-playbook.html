What counts as an event

Definitions

Every number on this page comes from one event chain, applied to all twenty Goldbach levels of the R=81 block and mirrored for both directions:

  1. Break. A close crosses the level.
  2. Excursion. Price then travels at least 1 ATR beyond it, and this must complete on a bar before the return.
  3. Retest. Price comes back to touch the level, within 240 bars.
  4. Resolution. The trade is scored against ATR-scaled barriers, from the bar after entry.

Barriers are ATR-scaled throughout because price ran from 1,722 to 30,976 across the sample — a fixed point target would measure the era, not the setup.

Two artifacts had to be removed first, and both flattered the setup badly.

The excursion flag was originally updated before the return was tested, which let a single wide bar count as break, excursion and retest at once. 54% of events qualified that way, each already a full ATR onside at its supposed entry — and no order could have been resting at the level, because the filter that selects the setup is only known once that bar has closed.

The entry bar was also scored symmetrically. A long fills at the level because that bar traded down to it, so the bar's high happened before the fill and counting it as profit is look-ahead, while its low is real heat. The entry bar can now stop you out but cannot pay you.

Together they were worth +0.19 ATR per trade of pure fiction: the baseline went from a 59.4% win rate and 17 profitable years out of 17, to 47.7% and none. Anything below is post-correction.

You will get a second entry

Usable

The single most valuable number here, and the one least sensitive to how the test is built — it involves no barriers, no fills and no assumptions about execution. Just: does price come back?

Do not chase the break. Four times in five the level comes back to you inside two hours, and the rate barely moves across block sizes — it is a property of how price moves, not of this lattice, which is exactly why you can rely on it.

The corollary matters as much: the one time in six that price never comes back is the move you would have caught by chasing. Sizing has to survive missing those.

The trade itself

Not an edge

Nothing moves it. The four named zones land within 0.3 percentage points of each other. An aligned liquidity sweep is worth less than a point. Confluence with the higher-timeframe R=729 lattice is worth nothing at all.

The one condition that clearly does move the number is the New York open hour — and it moves it down, by nearly three points. That is the hour with the widest bars, so the structure stop is placed furthest from entry and the 2R target furthest away again.

Reject or break?

Watch out

The plainest question, and the one your screen-time intuition is answering. Price arrives at a level having come from at least 1 ATR away. Scored as a fade filled at the line, with the arriving bar's push past it counted as heat against you.

Roughly three arrivals in five push through rather than turn. Read that as an execution fact rather than a market law: a half-ATR bracket sits inside the penetration distribution from earlier, so the number describes what happens to an order resting on the line, not what price "wants" to do.

And here the lattice finally shows something real. Two independent statistics, consistent direction. That is not noise.

It is also about a third of a percentage point. The base rate it sits on is ten points underwater, and one point of round-trip cost at current volatility is worth twenty times more than the effect. It is real, and it is not a trade.

All eight scales at once

Real, doesn't pay

Everything above takes one block size at a time. The tool on the chart does not — it shows all eight PO3 scales together, and the reported experience is that some levels work and others do nothing. Scale agreement is the obvious candidate for what separates them, and unlike most confluence stories it is exactly computable, because the blocks nest: 729 = 3 × 243 = 9 × 81.

First, the lattice itself. Every one of the eight rows in the tool's table reproduces from floor(price / R) × R to the point — 3, 9, 27, 81, 243, 729, 2187 and 6561. That is worth stating plainly: the levels are exactly what this study has been testing all along, so the null results are about the levels themselves, not about a bad reconstruction.

Major swings do not cluster where scales agree: 11 cells, z from -0.44 to +1.50, nothing close.

Reaction was a different story. Counting how many of the other five scales mark the same price, the rejection rate climbs monotonically — and shifted lattices, which preserve the nesting exactly and move only where it sits, stay flat:

It survived every confound. Deep confluence is not nearer round numbers — it is slightly further; excluding everything within 10 points of a round hundred makes the effect stronger; a round-number map sorts touches the opposite way; the small scales carry it rather than proximity to a 2187 or 6561 level; it holds in all three eras; and resampling whole days puts the advantage at +1.46pp, 95% CI [+0.93, +2.00], with every one of 2,000 resamples above zero.

Then it dissolved. Confluence depth is entangled with which level you are on: 81 = 3 × 27 means some percentages are structurally better connected than others. Holding the level fixed and comparing high against low confluence within it, the effect is worth +0.15 percentage points, and only 12 of 20 levels move the right way. It was level identity all along.

Which turns out to be a far bigger effect, and the strongest thing in this study. Shuffling the level labels within each trading day — keeping every outcome and all the intraday clustering, changing only which line each touch belongs to — none of 2,000 permutations reached the observed spread across the twenty levels. z = +10.79.

Among the eighteen, dispersion is weak (z +3.52) and does not cohere: the named Goldbach Inversion Point at 17/83 is among the weakest lines on the board, and mirror pairs that the framework says are equivalent agree only to within their error bars.

Your instinct to trade the extreme and the equilibrium is right, and this is the first evidence in the study that supports any part of the framework. But it is the exact boundary and the exact midpoint doing the work, not the 3-point band around them: the 0 line rejects 41.35% while the 3 line rejects only 39.49%.

R=81 no longer resolves

Watch out

Independent of every result above, this is the finding most likely to be costing you money right now. A block is only usable if it is large relative to a candle. The 3% zone — your 0.97–1.00 band — is a fixed 2.43 points at R=81, forever. The candle is not fixed.

In 2026 the R=81 zone is 0.15 of one candle. You are marking a band two and a half points wide on a chart whose median minute bar is nearly sixteen points, and price crosses almost five whole blocks in a session. There is nothing there to react to — the zone is finer than the instrument's own resolution.

In 2012 that same zone was nearly two candles wide and price crossed a third of a block a day. The framework was built when 81 meant something.

R=729 is where 81 used to sit. Its zone is 21.9 points — about 1.4 candles — and it covers roughly half a session's range. If you want the levels to be things price can plausibly respond to, that is the block. R=243 is the floor.

The cost hurdle

Arithmetic

Assume 0.45 points round trip — roughly $4 commission plus a tick of slippage on the stop exit, with entries and targets as resting limits. Against a structure trade risking 6.5 points, that is 6.9% of your risk gone before the trade starts.

To clear it at 2R you need 35.6% rather than 33.3%. The measured rate is 30.7%. The gap is not close, and it does not close under any condition tested.

What this does not cover

Honest limits

Your actual entry is a confirmation signal — tCISD, an inverted fair-value gap, a market-structure shift. The structure entry tested here is the closest mechanical proxy for it, but it is not your signal. If the edge lives in the confirmation rather than in the level, nothing on this page can see it, and I cannot rule that out.

What this page does establish is narrower and still worth having: the lines alone carry no edge. Any edge you have is in the reading, not the grid — which means it is worth knowing whether the reading survives measurement.

To test that, I need one of two things:

  • 20–30 setups you marked yourself, with date and time, so your swing-marking can be compared against the detectors used here; or
  • a log of 50–100 actual trades — entry, stop, target, outcome.

What to do differently

Desk card
  • 01Move to R=729. R=81's zone is below the resolution of a 2026 candle. R=243 is the minimum.
  • 02Never chase a break. You get the level back 84.3% of the time within two hours.
  • 03Stop at least 0.75 ATR beyond the line, not at it. Median push-through is 0.32 ATR.
  • 04Stop expecting the zone to add a filter. EXT, EQ, FV and GIP perform identically, as does sweep context and HTF confluence.
  • 05Trade wider than 2R or not at all. Every tighter ratio is below its break-even before costs.
  • 06Treat the levels as a map, not a signal. They tell you where you are. They do not tell you what happens next.
