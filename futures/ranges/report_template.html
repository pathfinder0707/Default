Goldbach Ranges
on the Lattice

The Goldbach Trifecta anchors its dealing range on a fixed power-of-three price lattice. That anchoring is exact, recoverable, and testable — so it was tested, six ways, against three matched nulls, on sixteen years of real CME futures spanning a 18-fold change in price. Nothing survived.

01

What the framework actually specifies

The source document is a discretionary trade-plan narrative. It names its levels but never states how the dealing range is anchored — which is the whole game, since every level is a percentage of it.

That anchoring was recovered from the chart images. One chart carries a line explicitly labelled [729 L], the block low. It reads 25,515. And 25,515 = 35 × 729, exactly.

The dealing range is a fixed price lattice. block_low = floor(price / R) * R, with R a power of three. Not swing-anchored, not session-anchored — absolute price.

That single hypothesis then predicts every other plotted line across all four charts, to within a pixel or two. Four independent block anchors all land exactly on the lattice: 10×2187, 11×2187, 35×729.

The clincher is that the author's own annotations land where the reconstruction puts them — EXECUTE on 17, RETRACE on 29, REBALANCE on 50, LAST LINE OF DEFENSE on 93. Those labels were not used to fit anything.

02

How it was tested

Hypotheses, statistics, nulls and decision rules were written down and frozen before any of them was computed. Only the data sanity checks ran first, and those are not hypothesis-dependent.

The confound, and the fix

“Is this price on a level” is deterministic given the price. So the question is never do swings sit on levels — some always will, at the rate the coverage implies. The question is whether they sit there more than they would on a lattice that is identical in every respect except its origin.

Three nulls, deliberately orthogonal. A finding had to survive all three.

  • Occupancy null. Swings only form where price goes. Compares against the block-position distribution of every bar extreme in the data.
  • Phase null. The same measurement under 60 lattice origins, offset by j·R/60. The true lattice is one of them. This is the decisive one — it kills “price respects round numbers in general.”
  • Level-set null. Takes the framework's own multiset of gaps — {3,4,4,6,…} — permutes the order and rotates. Every draw matches GB exactly on level count, gap distribution and coverage, so the only thing that differs is which specific numbers are levels.

Two traps carried over from the GB-time run. There, a +4.86σ result at minute :00 turned out to be a round-clock artifact, and a +10.84σ sequencing result came from a null that destroyed chronology and manufactured its own signal. The price analogues — round decimal prices, and chronology-breaking shuffles — were controlled for here from the start.

Test one, everywhere else

The primary configuration was fixed in advance, but a single configuration proves little. The same test across four block sizes, four swing strengths and three tolerances — 48 cells, 144 z-scores.

On this much data the grid does throw up large numbers: {{grid_over3}} of {{n_grid_z}} z-scores exceed |3|, the largest {{grid_max}}. Taken individually several would read as findings.

The pre-registration required clearing +3σ against every null, not whichever one happens to cooperate. On that test, {{grid_survive}} cells survive. Every large positive is the occupancy null at R=729 — and each one carries a negative phase z, meaning lattices with a shifted origin score higher than the true Goldbach one. Whatever mild structure exists at that block size, it is not a property of this lattice's phase.

Test two — does a level produce a reaction?

Swings landing on levels is indirect. The question a trader faces is narrower: price is arriving at a level — does it reject or go through?

On a deduplicated first touch approached from below, reject means price travels further down than up over the next N bars. With symmetric excursion that is the same as asking which side of a bracket around the level gets hit first.

08

Limitations, stated plainly

  • The basis limitation is resolved. The earlier run used HistData's cash Nasdaq-100 CFD, and the cash-futures basis meant its lattice phase was only loosely tied to the real one. This run uses genuine CME NQ front-month futures, unadjusted, rolled by daily volume. Over the overlap the two feeds differed by +96 to +143 points, drifting by year. That objection no longer applies to any result here.
  • Sixteen years is a much harder test than three. Price ran from 1,722 to 30,976, an 18-fold change. A lattice anchored on absolute price means something completely different at each end — a 243-point block was 14% of price in 2010 and 0.8% in 2026. If the anchoring carried information, this is where it would have to show.
  • The trade plans are discretionary. "Price should aggressively reject" and "look for potential gap" cannot be coded without inventing rules the author did not specify. What was tested is the levels and the structural claims — whether the map has any territory under it. Not one particular trader's execution on top of it.
  • Absence of evidence at this resolution. The power analysis bounds what was ruled out: effects smaller than roughly one percentage point of reject rate, or one percent of swings, would not have been seen. Those are below the size that survives spread.
  • One instrument. Sixteen years of Nasdaq futures. The framework claims to describe CME-driven price cycles generally.
09

Conclusion

The pre-registration set three conditions for GB-Ranges to count as working: clear +3σ against all three nulls, survive the round-decimal check, and appear in more than one block size or year.

None of the six claims cleared the first condition. Of {{n_grid_z}} grid z-scores, {{grid_survive}} clear +3σ against all three nulls. The level-reaction test returns 0.500 at every level, block size and horizon. The boundary sweep behaves identically for Goldbach edges and shifted edges — while the round-decimal control, on the same statistic and the same data, reads {{round_best_z}}.

That last contrast is what makes this a measurement rather than a shrug. The test can see line-specific behaviour. It sees it at round numbers. It does not see it at Goldbach levels.

The lattice reconstruction itself remains solid and worth keeping — the anchoring rule is exact and the chart evidence for it is unambiguous. What the data does not support is the idea that price behaves any differently on arriving there.

The levels are real in the sense that they are exactly computable and the framework's own charts confirm them. They are not real in the sense of price doing anything different when it arrives.
