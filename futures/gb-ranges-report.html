Goldbach Ranges
on the Lattice

The Goldbach Trifecta anchors its dealing range on a fixed power-of-three price lattice. That anchoring is exact, recoverable, and testable — so it was tested, six ways, against three matched nulls, on sixteen years of real CME futures spanning a 18-fold change in price. Nothing survived.

01

What the framework actually specifies

The source document is a discretionary trade-plan narrative. It names its levels but never states how the dealing range is anchored — which is the whole game, since every level is a percentage of it.

That anchoring was recovered from the chart images. One chart carries a line explicitly labelled [729 L], the block low. It reads 25,515. And 25,515 = 35 × 729, exactly.

The dealing range is a fixed price lattice. block_low = floor(price / R) * R, with R a power of three. Not swing-anchored, not session-anchored — absolute price.

That single hypothesis then predicts every other plotted line across all four charts, to within a pixel or two. Four independent block anchors all land exactly on the lattice: 10×2187, 11×2187, 35×729.

The clincher is that the author's own annotations land where the reconstruction puts them — EXECUTE on 17, RETRACE on 29, REBALANCE on 50, LAST LINE OF DEFENSE on 93. Those labels were not used to fit anything.

The twenty levels

Percentages of the block, symmetric about 50. At the tested tolerance of ±0.5 percentage points they cover exactly 20.0% of every block — a genuinely falsifiable target, unlike the GB-time settings that made 97.6% of the day a node.

02

How it was tested

Hypotheses, statistics, nulls and decision rules were written down and frozen before any of them was computed. Only the data sanity checks ran first, and those are not hypothesis-dependent.

The confound, and the fix

“Is this price on a level” is deterministic given the price. So the question is never do swings sit on levels — some always will, at the rate the coverage implies. The question is whether they sit there more than they would on a lattice that is identical in every respect except its origin.

Three nulls, deliberately orthogonal. A finding had to survive all three.

  • Occupancy null. Swings only form where price goes. Compares against the block-position distribution of every bar extreme in the data.
  • Phase null. The same measurement under 60 lattice origins, offset by j·R/60. The true lattice is one of them. This is the decisive one — it kills “price respects round numbers in general.”
  • Level-set null. Takes the framework's own multiset of gaps — {3,4,4,6,…} — permutes the order and rotates. Every draw matches GB exactly on level count, gap distribution and coverage, so the only thing that differs is which specific numbers are levels.

Two traps carried over from the GB-time run. There, a +4.86σ result at minute :00 turned out to be a round-clock artifact, and a +10.84σ sequencing result came from a null that destroyed chronology and manufactured its own signal. The price analogues — round decimal prices, and chronology-breaking shuffles — were controlled for here from the start.

Test one — do swings land on levels?

If the levels are real decision points, confirmed turning points should sit on them more often than chance. This is the foundational claim: everything the trade plans describe is built on it.

The hit rate is 0.2041 against a coverage of 0.2000. Held against a naive uniform null that is z = +6.94 — which, taken at face value, looks like an emphatic confirmation of the framework.

It is not, and this is the whole reason the occupancy null was pre-registered. Swings can only form where price actually goes. The same measurement over every bar extreme in the data gives 0.2042 — indistinguishable from the swing rate. Control for where price spends its time and z = +6.94 becomes z = -0.18. The entire apparent effect was the shape of the price distribution, not the levels.

04

Test one, everywhere else

The primary configuration was fixed in advance, but a single configuration proves little. The same test across four block sizes, four swing strengths and three tolerances — 48 cells, 144 z-scores.

On this much data the grid does throw up large numbers: 13 of 144 z-scores exceed |3|, the largest +5.53. Taken individually several would read as findings.

The pre-registration required clearing +3σ against every null, not whichever one happens to cooperate. On that test, 0 cells survive. Every large positive is the occupancy null at R=729 — and each one carries a negative phase z, meaning lattices with a shifted origin score higher than the true Goldbach one. Whatever mild structure exists at that block size, it is not a property of this lattice's phase.

05

Test two — does a level produce a reaction?

Swings landing on levels is indirect. The question a trader faces is narrower: price is arriving at a level — does it reject or go through?

On a deduplicated first touch approached from below, reject means price travels further down than up over the next N bars. With symmetric excursion that is the same as asking which side of a bracket around the level gets hit first.

08

Tested as actually traded

Everything above tests the framework as written. A trader using it objected, reasonably, that this is not what they do: they use three zones rather than all twenty levels, only in context, and they judge the levels by where major swings form — not by whether a touch predicts direction. Three further tests followed, each validated on a positive control first.

Does price stop at the level?

Direction-agnostic, and the strongest remaining version of the claim: a level can be worthless for prediction and still be real support. Measured as how far price overshoots a line before turning back.

A control in which the levels were made true reflecting barriers cut median penetration by 93%. The observed difference is about 1%, in the wrong direction. And overshoot is ~23 points at both block sizes — it is set by the instrument's noise scale, not by which line was drawn.

Do major swings form in the named zones?

The earlier swing test used pivots of at most 20 bars a side — a 40-minute local pivot, not a major swing — and pooled all twenty levels. This uses percentage-reversal zigzags (the 1% threshold gives 5,982 swings in 16 years, about 1.7 a session) and pivots up to 480 bars a side, scoring only the three named zones. Across 128 cells, z runs -2.17 to +2.27.

One cell deserves care rather than dismissal. At R=2187 the range-extreme zone is elevated by roughly 40% over the shifted-phase null, consistently across all eight swing definitions — exactly the pattern a real effect would produce.

The elevation is itself a small-sample artifact: only 13.4 blocks of 2187 span the whole 16-year price range, so block-position occupancy reflects where price happened to sit rather than any property of the grid. Phase-to-phase scatter in that occupancy is 0.0117 at R=2187 against 0.0021 at R=81 — the signature of too few blocks. The eight agreeing rows are one observation, not eight: overlapping swing sets measuring the same lump.

At R=81, the block actually used on a 1–5 minute chart, every zone and swing-definition cell falls between z -1.99 and +0.58.

Does a broken level hold as support on the retest?

The mechanism the source document actually describes, and the one every test above misses. The trade plans say explicitly not to trade the first touch: "you don't buy at this level, but anticipate a rejection ... when price indeed moves through the flow layer, you still don't buy ... they will first retrace price, back into the flow layer. When price retraces, this is where you buy." Einstein describes the same shape, and [17–83] is named the Goldbach Inversion Point.

The event chain is strict, so ordinary oscillation around a line cannot satisfy it: cross the level, travel at least 1 ATR beyond it, return to touch it, then resolve whether price resumes the break direction by 0.5 ATR before losing the same the other way.

Corrected. An earlier version of this section reported a 0.5734 hold rate and called it the largest real effect in the study. That number was an artifact of two measurement errors, both since fixed, and the section below carries the corrected result.

First, the excursion flag was updated before testing for the return, so a single wide bar could count as break, excursion and retest at once. Fifty-four percent of events qualified that way, each already a full ATR onside at its supposed entry — and no order could have been resting at the level, because the filter that selects the event is only known once that bar has closed. Second, the retest bar was scored symmetrically: a long fills at the level because that bar traded down to it, so the bar's far side happened before the fill and counting it as profit is look-ahead, while the part beyond the level is real heat. Corrected, the entry bar can stop you out but cannot pay you.

Together they were worth about 17 percentage points of hold rate.

That is not a statement about Goldbach. It is what a symmetric bracket placed inside a level's own noise does: the retest bar routinely penetrates far enough to take out a half-ATR stop before anything resumes.

The lattice's own contribution is separately visible and separately tiny. The shifted-lattice null sits about 0.47 percentage points below the true phase, and here the sign is at last consistent — the true lattice ranks 1 of 20 at three of the four block sizes. The full setup, in context, with confirmation

The three zones, on R=81/243/729, conditional on a liquidity sweep or a higher-timeframe fair-value-gap tap, with and without a change-in-state confirmation, scored on whether price then travels +1 ATR before −1 ATR. 96 cells, each against its own 20-phase lattice null.

Median win rate across all cells is 0.497 and median expectancy -0.006R. The best cell (R=81, EQ 50, after a sweep) reaches 0.515 and +0.030R at z = +3.01 — the ceiling that 96 draws from noise produce, and short of the Bonferroni threshold of 3.47.

A look-ahead bug in the first run of this test was doing all the work. The bracket resolved from the entry bar inclusive, but entry is at that bar's close, so its range had already happened. That inflated the headline cell from a 49.3% win rate to 65.2%, and its expectancy from −0.015R to +0.305R. It is recorded here because a 65% win rate at 1:1 is exactly what a real edge feels like, and it was an artifact of one wrong index.

The surviving cell is also far below cost. Risk is one 1-minute ATR, whose sample median is 4.72 points, so a single point of round-trip cost is 0.212R — about seven times the best edge measured.

09

Limitations, stated plainly

  • The basis limitation is resolved. The earlier run used HistData's cash Nasdaq-100 CFD, and the cash-futures basis meant its lattice phase was only loosely tied to the real one. This run uses genuine CME NQ front-month futures, unadjusted, rolled by daily volume. Over the overlap the two feeds differed by +96 to +143 points, drifting by year. That objection no longer applies to any result here.
  • Sixteen years is a much harder test than three. Price ran from 1,722 to 30,976, an 18-fold change. A lattice anchored on absolute price means something completely different at each end — a 243-point block was 14% of price in 2010 and 0.8% in 2026. If the anchoring carried information, this is where it would have to show.
  • The trade plans are discretionary. "Price should aggressively reject" and "look for potential gap" cannot be coded without inventing rules the author did not specify. What was tested is the levels and the structural claims — whether the map has any territory under it. Not one particular trader's execution on top of it.
  • Absence of evidence at this resolution. The power analysis bounds what was ruled out: effects smaller than roughly one percentage point of reject rate, or one percent of swings, would not have been seen. Those are below the size that survives spread.
  • One instrument. Sixteen years of Nasdaq futures. The framework claims to describe CME-driven price cycles generally.
10

Conclusion

The pre-registration set three conditions for GB-Ranges to count as working: clear +3σ against all three nulls, survive the round-decimal check, and appear in more than one block size or year.

None of the six claims cleared the first condition. Of 144 grid z-scores, 0 clear +3σ against all three nulls. The level-reaction test returns 0.500 at every level, block size and horizon. The boundary sweep behaves identically for Goldbach edges and shifted edges — while the round-decimal control, on the same statistic and the same data, reads -3.39.

That last contrast is what makes this a measurement rather than a shrug. The test can see line-specific behaviour. It sees it at round numbers. It does not see it at Goldbach levels.

Break-and-retest — the one mechanism the source document actually asks you to trade — does not rescue it either. Corrected for two measurement artifacts that had inflated it by 17 points, the retest holds 0.4037 of the time, and entered properly at the close with the stop beyond the retest bar's wick it is a martingale: exactly break-even at 3R before costs, negative at every tighter ratio.

The lattice does leave one consistent fingerprint. Across the touch test and the retest test, at every block size, the true phase beats its shifted twins by roughly a third of a percentage point, and ranks first of twenty more often than chance allows. That is the honest positive result of this study: real, reproducible, and about thirty times too small to pay for a spread.

The lattice reconstruction itself remains solid and worth keeping — the anchoring rule is exact and the chart evidence for it is unambiguous. What the data does not support is the idea that price behaves any differently on arriving there.

The levels are real in the sense that they are exactly computable and the framework's own charts confirm them. They are not real in the sense of price doing anything different when it arrives.
