The verdict, up front. There is no PO3 edge to utilise. A power of three is not a special block size — the results vary smoothly with size, and non-PO3 values beat every PO3 value. The one setup that showed a strong paper edge (+3.32 pts/trade, t = 9.63) was earning it entirely from an optimistic limit-fill assumption, and goes negative the moment fills are modelled honestly.

Sixteen years did surface one thing three years could not, in section 5: the paper edge is a post-2020 regime, not a constant. And section 1 now shows the absolute-price lattice going stale in the clearest possible terms — the block matching a median day moved 27 → 81 → 243 across the sample.

The drift problem

Over sixteen years NQ's median RTH range went from 23.8 to 392.8 points — 16.5× — while price rose from 1,722 to 30,976. Range as a percentage of price barely moved at all: 0.66% to 2.34%, averaging 1.24%. Volatility scales with price; a fixed point-grid does not. That is fatal for a lattice anchored on absolute price.

YearDaysMedian close Median rangeRange % of price P(range ≤ 243)P(range ≤ 729)

The block matching a median day moved 27 → 81 → 243 across the sample, a 9× drift. P(day range ≤ 27) was 68% in 2010 and 0% from 2018 onward. P(day range ≤ 243) held at 100% through 2017 and is 13.4% in 2026. Each power of three is the right day-block for roughly five to eight years, then the market outgrows it.

04

How the edge died

S2 assumed a resting limit fills every time a bar's range touches the level. But the trades that make the money are exactly the ones where price tags the level and reverses — and those are the fills you are most likely to miss, because price never traded through your order. Everyone ahead of you in the queue gets filled; you don't.

So require price to trade a given distance beyond the level before counting a fill. The entry price stays the same. Only the fill assumption tightens.

ConfigPenetrationTrades Win %Exp ptst Total pts

Every point of required penetration costs roughly 1.2 points of expectancy, decaying monotonically through zero. That is not a strategy with a cost problem — that is the shape of a result made entirely of adverse selection. On real futures it is gone after one point (+0.17, t = 1.27) and negative by two.

One point is a gentle assumption on an instrument that ticks in 0.25s. It is roughly the minimum you should assume if you are not co-located and near the front of the queue. Note this died faster here than on the cash CFD tested previously, where it survived to two points — real futures have tighter spreads and punish the assumption harder.

All five time-of-day windows are negative

On the cash data the opening hour was the last survivor at +1.90 (t = 3.62). On real futures, with a 2-point fill requirement, it is −0.22 (t = −1.01) — and every other window is worse.

05

What sixteen years showed that three could not

The single genuinely new finding here. The paper edge is not a constant property of the market — it is a regime that began around 2020.

EraTradesPaper exp Years positiveAfter 2pt fill
2010–20191,833 −0.642 of 10 −2.34
2020–20264,565 +2.157 of 7 −0.07

Level mean-reversion genuinely became far more prevalent after 2020. Seven years out of seven positive on paper is not noise — something in market structure changed.

But apply realistic fills and the post-2020 era lands at −0.07 points per trade. Breakeven, not profitable. The regime shift is real; it is simply not large enough to clear the cost of getting filled. Worth knowing the effect exists. Not worth trading.

This also reframes the earlier three-year study: it happened to sample only the favourable regime, which is why its paper numbers looked better (+3.32 vs +1.40 here) while failing the same fill test.

07

What to actually do

Don't trade the PO3 lattice

Nothing here supports it. The lattice phase is irrelevant, no power of three is a peak in any sweep, and the only strategy that looked profitable was extracting its edge from a fill assumption rather than from the market.

If you keep the blocks on your chart, size them like this

At 2026 volatility, not the sixteen-year pooled average:

  • Scalping, 1–15m: R = 27.
  • 30–60m structure: R = 81.
  • Session / 4h: R = 243.
  • Containing the whole day: R = 729 — it holds 92.6% of 2026 days, while 243 holds only 13.4%.

Better: stop using a fixed number at all. Set spacing to roughly 0.5–0.6× the trailing 20-day median RTH range — around 200–240 NQ points at current volatility. That tracks the market instead of going stale every five to eight years, which is what the drift table shows a fixed power of three doing. It still is not an edge; it is just the non-broken way to place lines.

The transferable lesson

This is the most valuable thing in the report. Any backtest where you enter with a limit order at a level will overstate its edge, because it assumes you are filled on exactly the touches that reverse instantly. Before believing any such result, re-run it requiring price to trade 1–3 points through your level. If the edge does not survive that, it was never there. This one lost all of its expectancy to that single test — on both a cash CFD and real futures, across nineteen years of combined data.

What would change the answer

  • Tick or full-depth data. This now runs on genuine CME NQ futures, so the cash-basis objection is closed. What remains is the fill model: the penetration test is a proxy for queue position, and it is doing all the work in the verdict. One month of 1-second or market-by-order data would replace that estimate with a measurement.
  • Tick or depth data. It would replace the fill assumption with an actual queue model rather than a penetration proxy.
  • An execution rule the source document actually specifies. The trade plans are discretionary — "price should aggressively reject", "look for potential gap". What is tested here is the structure those plans stand on, not any one trader's reading of them.
